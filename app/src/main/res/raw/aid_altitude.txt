! Confused, very drowsy, or stumbling like they are drunk: brain swelling (HACE). Test: have them walk heel to toe in a straight line. If they can't, treat it as brain swelling.
! Short of breath at rest, a cough, or too weak to keep up: fluid in the lungs (HAPE).
! Either one: go down now, even at night if the way down is safe, with people helping them. Call 911 or your local emergency number. No signal: try 911 anyway (see Calling for help); if it fails, start down with them. If there are enough of you, send two people ahead for help.
  Go down at least 1,000 to 3,000 ft (300 to 1,000 m), or until they are clearly better.
  Keep them warm. Let them do as little as they can: carry their pack. If they can't go down yet, keep them warm and resting, sitting up if breathing is hard, and go down as soon as you can.
  Even if they feel better lower down, end the trip and get them to a doctor. Don't go back up.
# Mild altitude sickness
- Headache, plus nausea, poor appetite, dizziness, tiredness or poor sleep
1. Don't go any higher until the symptoms are gone.
2. Rest. Drink water. No alcohol or sleeping pills.
3. A pain reliever they already use may ease the headache.
4. Worse, or not better after a day of rest: go down. Going down 1,000 to 3,000 ft (300 to 1,000 m) usually helps. Keep going until they feel better.
+++
# When
- Mostly above 8,000 ft (2,500 m). Mild signs often start 2 to 12 hours after going higher.
- Lung fluid (HAPE) usually starts on the 2nd to 4th night up high. Brain swelling (HACE) can follow days of mild symptoms.
# Prevent it
- Go up slowly. Above 10,000 ft (3,000 m), sleep no more than 1,600 ft (500 m) higher each night, with a rest day every 3 or 4 days.
- Being fit does not protect you. Anyone can get it.
# Don't
- Don't blame a headache, cough or tiredness up high on a cold, a hangover or the hike. Treat it as altitude sickness until proven otherwise.
- Don't go higher with symptoms.
- Don't send someone sick down alone.
